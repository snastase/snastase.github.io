### November 27, 2026 &mdash; Open review of Murphy: 
Murphy, E. (2026). No country for old linguists: LLM-brain alignment underdetermines neural computation. *arXiv*. [`DOI`](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2609.03160)

---

In this short piece, Murphy outlines a handful of disagreements with a manuscript we recently published ([Nastase et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2026.07.024)). This review was solicited by a journal editor with full understanding that I am the first author of the original manuscript and as such may have a conflicting interest. With this in mind, I’ve written the review in consultation with my co-authors and formulated it more as a response to the comments than a traditional peer review. (My understanding is that Murphy’s critique will also be reviewed by other third-party reviewers.) 

We genuinely appreciate the author engaging with our work and taking the time to compose this response. Modern neural network models for language reproduce complex, human-like language behavior in a way that other models do not. It is this behavioral alignment first and foremost that elevates their position among the models under consideration. Linear encoding provides evidence of model–brain alignment, but is only one piece of the puzzle, as we discuss below. To our reading, the author’s goal is to narrow the space of hypotheses to those furnished by formal linguistics, aiming to rule out the computations furnished by neural networks a priori. We believe that narrowing the space of possible models in this way is not theoretically or empirically justified. In the following, we respond to what we perceive as the author’s main critiques in comments 1–5, then close with a concluding statement; we include a few minor reviewing comments toward the end.

1a. We believe the central point of disagreement stems from a narrow reading of our arguments. Murphy characterizes our arguments as following a particular structure, roughly: “LLMs provide strong predictions of brain activity; therefore LLMs identify the brain mechanisms for language processing.” This characterization of our arguments is front-and-center in the Title (“LLM–brain alignment”), in the Abstract (“Large language models (LLMs) have been argued to serve as candidate models of human language processing because their representations predict neural responses”), and in the Introduction (“The key question is what kind of inference LLM–brain alignment licenses”). There are two reasons why this reading does not fully reflect the structure or scope of our arguments, which we unpack below.

1b. First, we do not argue that any particular result of model–brain alignment will conclusively identify or uniquely determine the neural mechanisms for language. In the Introduction, for example, Murphy states that representational alignment does not allow us to conclude that LLMs “might instantiate the same computational principles as biological brains.” This is true and we do not intend to exhaustively conclude that LLMs instantiate exactly the same computational principles as biological brains; in fact, we highlight several ways in which they clearly differ from biological brains in the original manuscript (e.g., “Conspicuous gaps between artificial and biological neural networks”). This jump from evidence of model–brain alignment to the conclusive identification of mechanism does not accurately represent our arguments, our interpretation of the results, or the broad interpretational stance in the surrounding literature. As we explain in response to later comments, we believe that this deductive framing is an oversimplification and that our strongest evidence for convergence stems from theoretically motivated model comparisons.

1c. Second, Murphy’s characterization of our argument positions the prediction performance of linear encoding models as the evidentiary focus, largely ignoring the other elements of our argument. We do not argue that LLMs “serve as candidate models of human language processing” based solely on the observation that “their representations predict neural responses”—as the abstract implies. Rather, we outline a variety of ways in which LLMs (like other families of artificial neural networks) share a number of important properties with biological neural networks. For example, their internal representations are *learned* incrementally via simple input/output interactions with an external environment of text (rather than being hand-designed or innately endowed); they are “input-computable” in that they receive natural language tokens as input and generate plausible tokens as output; the full causal chain from input to output is mechanistically specified (in contrast to purely descriptive models); their internal representations are distributed across simple, neuron-like computing elements where individual artificial neurons tend to exhibit heterogeneous, usage-oriented tuning; they encode behaviorally relevant structures (for language) in the geometric relations between patterns of internal activity. These are widely observed and well established principles of neural encoding in biological systems; these principles are also theoretically desirable for their computational simplicity and biological plausibility. We also revisit the argument that LLMs provide an existence proof that broad linguistic competence can arise in a system that does not rely on the algebraic–symbolic constructs previously thought to be necessary for language (e.g., Piantadosi, 2024; [Weissweiler et al., 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.cxgsnlp-1.7.pdf)), raising the possibility that the brain might not rely on these constructs either. These features of LLMs elevate their position as candidate models of human language processing based on theoretical considerations without relying directly on their (strong) prediction performance.

2a. Murphy points out that the linear regression framework used with typical encoding models does not “establish that the two representational spaces share a geometry” because the learned encoding weight matrix “is free to shear, rescale, and collapse dimensions.” This is true and we believe this point is well understood by much of the readership. As the author notes in his “zero-shot” comment, our team has also made efforts to more explicitly test whether linear encoding models can reconstruct neural representational geometry by (a) evaluating model predictions based on pattern similarity (b) for strictly left-out words in left-out contexts ([Goldstein et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-46631-y)); these results provide stronger evidence for geometric alignment and show that LLM embeddings can reconstruct neural representational geometry considerably better than other models ([Goldstein et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-46631-y); Figs. S6–S7). We regret if at any point in the original manuscript our writing slides directly from linear encoding to claims of geometric alignment. We reiterate, however, that many of our claims in the original manuscript are not that any particular measurements of brain activity share a metric geometry with any particular LLM activities, but rather that both biological neural systems and artificial neural networks seem to converge on geometric encoding schemes with many shared properties. As mentioned in the comment above, this latter claim rests on a variety of different observations, not just evidence of linear encoding performance.

2b. Linear regression, despite learning a very simple mapping, is less constrained than Procrustes alignment, for instance, and does not preserve Euclidean distances (or angles) between samples in the model feature space. However, this raises the question of whether we should expect every model feature to be represented with equal strength in a given brain area and whether a Euclidean distance metric (or cosine, correlation, etc.) between model features should match the distances between activity patterns in the brain (both typical assumptions in alternative model-comparison methods like representational similarity analysis; RSA). Linear regression instead relaxes these constraints and provides a strong framework for comparing models where we might not expect metric pairwise distances to match. 

2c. Murphy adopts a fairly specific stance that “convergence on a representational format is a claim about shared geometry,” as if encoding models cannot provide evidence for one representational format over another. Some of the disagreement may stem from differing interpretations of terms like “shared information,” “shared representational format,” “shared geometry,” and “shared computational principles.” Linear encoding models measure shared information between a model feature space and brain activity. By using linear encoding to compare models with different geometries, or to compare models with geometric representational format to non-geometric (e.g., symbolic) models, we can adjudicate between different claims to shared representational format and shared geometry, despite the fact that encoding models do not expressly measure geometric match. Finally, we believe our argument for shared computational principles extends beyond just evidence from encoding models, as we describe elsewhere in the review (i.e., distributed population coding, learned context-sensitive representations, and transformations through high-dimensional state spaces). With theoretically motivated model comparisons and out-of-sample prediction across diverse samples, we believe linear encoding models can narrow the space of viable representational formats, even if it does not identify an isometric match. Advocates of algebraic–symbolic constructs are welcome to evaluate their preferred models against the growing variety of alternatives in this same framework of quantitative model comparison. In any event, we agree with the reviewer that different methods for measuring geometric alignment, privileged axes, and so forth are exciting directions for future work (e.g., [Williams et al., 2021](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2021/file/252a3dbaeb32e7690242ad3b556e626b-Paper.pdf); [Khosla et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-026-02515-3)).

3a. Murphy states that both the brain activity and model representations “are responses to the same structured linguistic stimulus” and that “apparent alignment may therefore be inherited from shared stimulus structure rather than shared computation.” We agree that certain features (e.g., temporal autocorrelation) in both the brain activity and model activity may tend to increase apparent encoding performance. This is one reason why, in recent papers, we (and others) compare neural network models against untrained versions of the same model, where the architecture and stimulus features are held constant (e.g., [Zada et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2506.20489)). However, there are several complications to bear in mind; we outline three below.

3b. First, we do not see how a “shared computation” can be evaluated without recourse to a shared input, making this concern endemic. We are likely in agreement that an important part of the research cycle is using carefully-designed “controversial” stimuli or experiments to tease apart models that otherwise perform similarly under naturalistic conditions ([Golan et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1912334117), [2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-023-00718-1), [2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-026-01070-0)). (We had included this point in a previous draft of the manuscript, but the passage was ultimately removed for space.)

3c. Second, attempting to fully divorce computation from input is ill-advised if the computations of interest are tightly yoked to the structures of the stimuli/inputs ([Hasson et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2019.12.002); [Nastase et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2020.117254)). There is in fact a great deal of experimental work in both psychology and neuroscience to this effect. We should not assume that statistical properties of the stimulus, like autocorrelation, are strictly “confounds” when there is a large body of evidence suggesting that the brain is highly sensitive to these statistical features and leverages them for computation. With regard to the paper by Schönmann and colleagues ([2026](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.106543.3)), we have already provided our current perspective in the original manuscript (and are working on a longer-form response in a separate manuscript):

> “The nature of this pre-onset encoding, and to what extent it can be dissociated from stimulus-driven dependencies, remains a subject of active debate ([Schönmann et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.106543.3)). One clue may be the observation that pre-onset encoding does not appear to be a simple pre-activation of the post-onset word representation, since pre-onset predictions do not transfer directly to post-onset neural activity ([Azizpour et al., 2026]((https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.110085.1); [Schönmann et al, 2026](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.106543.3)). This pattern is consistent with the framework we propose here. We would not expect the brain to pre-activate a discrete representation of a specific upcoming word—that would require committing to a single prediction in advance. Instead, the brain likely maintains a continuous, distributed representation that simultaneously encodes the current context and probabilistically anticipates a range of features consistent with the likely upcoming words, without committing to one of them until the word actually arrives.”

3d. Third, we must reemphasize that we do not advocate for the author’s particular characterization of model–brain alignment. Our goal is not simply to show that one model has strong alignment with the brain and call it a day, as if this would conclusively identify a shared mechanism. Rather, our goal is to use systematic, theoretically motivated model comparisons to provide evidence one-way-or-another for certain model features. In this framework, typically all models under comparison are subject to the same stimuli and susceptible to the author’s concern. Our vision (and the field’s more broadly, we believe) is to build a cumulative science of theoretically motivated model comparisons that will ultimately converge on more brain-like mechanisms (e.g., [Richards et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-019-0520-2); [Doerig et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-023-00705-w); [Cao & Yamins, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogsys.2024.101244)). While this approach may use a variety of different methods for quantifying alignment between models and human behavior and brain activity (e.g., linear encoding models, representational similarity analysis), prediction performance is not the sole arbiter between models. Even models that yield similar brain alignment scores may be adjudicated based on their behavioral alignment and other theoretically desirable features (e.g., pursuing a model with simple, learnable features over a model that requires handcrafted features, even if the latter provides similarly strong predictions).

\4. Regarding the formal argument by Guest and Martin ([2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42113-022-00166-x)), we agree that observing “the model predicts human data” does not logically imply that “the model captures the human mechanism,” because many mechanisms could yield comparable prediction performance. However, this argument does not establish that prediction performance is evidentially irrelevant to mechanism; it only establishes that a given observation of alignment does not identify a mechanism with deductive certainty. Our arguments (like many others) hem more closely to an abductive approach, where observations of model–brain and model–behavior alignment—particularly theoretically motivated comparisons between particular models—can favor the mechanisms formalized in one model over another (which [Guest and Martin, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42113-022-00166-x), acknowledge). For example, in very coarse Bayesian terms, P(M|A) ∝ P(A|M)P(M), where A is an observation of model–brain alignment and M is a model containing candidate mechanisms; here, alignment supports M1 over M2 when P(A|M1) > P(A|M2). The theoretical implications of these comparisons can be tightened by ensuring that the models under comparison (e.g., M1 and M2) differ according to one targeted mechanism of interest, by designing stimuli/tasks that more precisely differentiate models, or by measuring alignment in different ways (e.g., behavioral alignment, linear brain alignment, geometric brain alignment). Murphy himself states in the conclusion that “predictive and representational success should constrain explanation, not count as explanation itself”; this essentially follows our own abductive approach, where “constrain” is understood probabilistically and evidence of alignment redistributes the plausibility of competing explanations. Formal arguments aside, we find it silly to imply that observing that one model predicts behavior and brain activity more accurately than another does not provide us *some* potentially useful evidence for homing in on more human-like models and mechanisms. (We find this especially relevant for modern neural network models of language, which exhibit radically more human-like language behavior than any previous family of models). If the argument is simply that this abductive process is hard, that it requires careful theory and methods, and that not all papers reach the same level of rigor—then we completely agree.

5a. We appreciate the author’s argument that the constructs of formal linguistics may provide hints into the neural mechanisms of language (Figure 1). We take a contrasting, deflationary approach to “linguistic mechanism,” where a broad range of linguistic structures can be encoded geometrically in a high-dimensional embedding space, and a broad range of linguistic operations can be implemented in nonlinear transformations between layers and probabilistic transitions from one token to the next. LLMs demonstrate that a remarkably wide array of linguistic structure and competence can be captured using these simple mechanisms. We agree that there is a great deal of work needed to better understand how neural population codes encode linguistic structure and implement linguistic operations and we tried our best to highlight examples of this work in both the mechanistic interpretability literature (e.g., [Elhage et al., 2021](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2021/framework/index.html), [Lindsey et al., 2025](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/attribution-graphs/biology.html)) and the computational neuroscience literature (e.g., [Panichello & Buschman, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03390-w); [Churchland & Shenoy, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-024-00796-z)). We agree with Murphy that decodability of linguistic structure from neural activity does not imply a causal role in computation; neural network models may provide a useful testbed here by allowing us to test causal relations in silico through interventions and ablations.

5b. The overall disagreement here seems to largely stem from different expectations about what kinds of explanations will ultimately capture the neural mechanisms of language. Murphy seems to prefer a particular family of representational structures and grants formal linguistic constructs a more privileged role in specifying the relations that neural mechanisms must preserve. We, on the other hand, suspect that the constructs of formal linguistics will not play a large explanatory role in understanding how language emerges from neural activity. Rather, we expect that the neural mechanisms for many different linguistic operations will boil down to learning nonlinear transformations between continuous, high-dimensional vector spaces of population activity that propel the system forward to produce linguistically well-formed and meaningful outputs. We admit in the original manuscript that the transformer is not a good architectural match (e.g., memory-limited recurrent architectures are surely a better fit), but argue that the broad computational principles described above are pointing us in the right direction.

To conclude, we would like to reiterate our excitement about the field entering a stage where we can build models that actually reproduce complex human behaviors like natural language. Behavioral alignment is paramount. A model of cognition that cannot reproduce “a genuine slab of human behavior” ([Newell, 1973](https://doi.org/10.1016/b978-0-12-170150-5.50012-3)) is fragmentary at best and misleading at worst. To put the same point in the language of the “contravariance principle”: handcrafted models fit to very particular tasks are under very little pressure to yield brain-like solutions; on the other hand, pushing models to solve more difficult, real-world tasks—like reproducing the full breadth of human language—exerts a strong pressure toward more brain-like solutions ([Cao & Yamins, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogsys.2024.101244); [Yamins & Nayebi, 2026](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2607.08561)). Behavioral alignment is only the first test, however: we want to know whether the internal computations that produce those behaviors align with human brain activity. Here, too, LLMs have shown stronger alignment than algebraic–symbolic models under the comparisons we’ve discussed, but we are the first to admit that they still have a long way to go. Linear encoding gives us one way to assess model–brain alignment, but the question of alignment is broader than any single metric. The limitations of linear encoding do not justify a retreat to the constructs of formal linguistics; rather, they motivate us to demand more of our models and evaluate them more rigorously. The standard we’ve adopted in recent work is to build more cognitively plausible models that learn directly from realistic human experience (e.g., [Raviv et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aeh9311)), and to test them against both human behavior and brain activity. In particular, we believe that efforts to explore recurrent architectures and furnish models with richer opportunities to learn from perception, action, and social interaction are fruitful directions for future research. 


### Minor comments:

Page 1: The author quotes our description of LLMs as “fully mechanistic models” disapprovingly. An LLM’s architecture is fully specified up to a random initialization; its learning rule and objective function are mathematically defined; in the case of open-data foundation models, even the training diet is fully specified; in an open-source model, every weight, every nonlinearity, every activation for any given input and output can be fully specified or interrogated. All of these features of the model are fully specified and numerically executable. The model is trained end-to-end and is fully “input-computable” in that it can take in completely natural text and generate textual outputs; the causal chain from input to output is numerically precise and fully determined. We, the authors of the original paper, cannot see how this could be characterized as anything other than “fully mechanistic.” We would not, however, argue that an LLM is a complete model of the brain’s mechanisms for language processing. If the author is seeking a sort of mechanism that is lower-dimensional and isomorphic to formal linguistics, we would agree that deep learning models generally do not fit that particular definition of “mechanism”; on the other hand, we would argue that the mechanisms deployed by deep learning are more parsimonious and biologically plausible than many alternatives.

Page 2: Please precisely define the terms used in the lexical surprisal equation. For example, how is the context ci term defined and quantified in practice?

Page 3: The author states that “No doubt distributional information can be an important cue, but the point here is that a cue is not the operation it constrains.” We note that the underlying neural operations are not known a priori and may in fact be distributional in nature, even if the experimental manipulation aims to isolate an ostensibly non-distributional construct. In other words, showing that neural activity covaries with a particular linguistic operation could also be a cue to a distributional neural computation.
